That yellow hull 🚢 is the Minnehaha, the restored Twin City Rapid Transit streetcar steamboat.

She was one of the express boats that started running Lake Minnetonka in 1906. Same yellow-and-red paint as the streetcars, so people coming out from the Twin Cities knew what they were boarding. Service stopped in 1926. She was raised from the bottom decades later, restored, and ran public cruises again for years from Excelsior.

Right now she's drydocked in Excelsior. The Lake Minnetonka Historical Society is working on a new launch site so she can get back on the water.

If you've spent time on Excelsior Bay, you've probably looked for that yellow hull without thinking about it.

ExcelsiorExcelsior BayLake MinnetonkaWater StreetThe CommonsSt. Albans BayGideon's Baysouth shore

Stand on the Excelsior shore and look out across Excelsior Bay and you can still feel why this town started here. George Bertram and the Excelsior Pioneer Association began building in 1853, after Governor Alexander Ramsey named Lake Minnetonka the year before. People call it the lake's first village. The village incorporated in 1877.

The Commons along that water were set aside early as a public pleasure ground — about thirteen acres that are still public. Downtown along Water Street sits in the Excelsior Commercial Historic District on the National Register, with paperwork dated September 3, 2021. Streetcar service reached Excelsior in 1906 and ran until 1932. For a long stretch this was where the city met the lake.

Excelsior Bay is the water right off downtown. St. Albans Bay and Gideon's Bay sit just around the shore. Same lake, different week-to-week feel depending on which water you're actually buying.

I keep an Excelsior page current when people want the real south-shore picture — Water Street, the Commons, which bay they're actually talking about — before we walk addresses. If Excelsior is already on your mind, that's a solid place to start.

Look at the people on that boardwalk, dressed up for an afternoon at the Tonka Bay Hotel with a steamer sitting out on the water. That was Echo Bay, right across from Big Island, and for a lot of summers that shore was one of the busiest spots on Lake Minnetonka.

The place opened in 1879 as the Lake Park Hotel, one of the big resort houses on the lake, and later picked up the Tonka Bay Hotel name. By the streetcar-boat years Twin City Rapid Transit was running it, and it closed for good in 1911. Tonka Bay became its own city in 1901. It's small, but it has more Lake Minnetonka shoreline than any other city its size, right where the upper and lower lake meet.

Before it was a club, this Crystal Bay hill was James J. Hill's Hotel Lafayette.

Opened July 2, 1882. Rail right to the hotel. About 300 lake-view rooms. Billed as the finest hotel west of New York City. Chester Arthur and Ulysses S. Grant stayed. In 1888 W. E. B. Du Bois worked there as a busboy. On Oct 4, 1897 the hotel burned in under an hour. Hill deeded the land; Lafayette Club followed in 1899. The Spanish Mission house in this frame is the 1924 rebuild after a second fire - not the 1882 hotel.

What Investors Should Know About Minnesota Tax-Forfeiture Properties

Recently I helped a client navigate an issue involving a tax-forfeited property purchased through a county auction in Saint Paul, Minnesota (Ramsey County).

Tax-forfeiture auctions can create opportunities for investors, but they require verification beyond what appears in county tax records.

In this case, the property had prior assessments from the City of Saint Paul, including demolition costs and property maintenance charges for grass and weed abatement. These costs were not visible in the county tax system where the property was auctioned.

Because the property had gone through the tax-forfeiture process, those charges should have been cleared when ownership transferred. However, shortly before closing on the resale, the assessments appeared again.

Rather than assume anything, we contacted both the county tax-forfeiture office and the city to verify the records. After reviewing the situation together, the city confirmed the charges should have been removed when the property entered forfeiture.

The issue was resolved, but it highlights an important lesson.

When purchasing tax-forfeited or auction properties in Minnesota, confirm records with both the county and the city, request written confirmation when charges are cleared, and document communication between parties.

County tax records are only part of the story.
