In March 1945, an Army engineer named Al Stender was running small plywood storm boats full of soldiers across the Rhine. He had no idea where they'd been built. Four men could carry one down to the water, and on its outboard it skimmed right over the river.

They came from the boat works on Wayzata Bay, a shop better known for fast mahogany runabouts. That February the Army had asked for a big order of storm boats, on a deadline a Minneapolis paper said looked impossible. The men at the boat works got them done on time, and then some.

The war ended, and Al came home. He needed work, and his brother got him a job at that same boat works, where they started him downstairs learning every part that goes into a Tonka-Craft. Minnetonka MillsMinnehaha CreekLake MinnetonkaMinnetonka

Not long after soldiers came to build Fort Snelling, a few young men from the fort pushed a canoe up a creek, curious where it led. Two of them were hardly more than boys. Dakota men went with them, and one local historian writes that it was the Dakota who told them a big lake waited up ahead.

They found it. For a while the creek even carried the name of one of those boys, Joseph Brown. Then the lake simply dropped out of the record. For nearly thirty years no one wrote down another visit, and the place almost faded from memory, except for the Dakota, who never stopped coming to hunt, fish and gather wild rice.

When settlers finally arrived, the territorial governor gave the lake its Dakota name, Minnetonka, big water, and Brown's Creek became Minnehaha. By 1910 a small mill village called Minnetonka Mills had grown up a little way down the creek, and a stone arch bridge carried wagons across the water there. That's the bridge in my painting, on a summer day, with the same creek those boys paddled still running underneath it, east to the Mississippi.

She was one of the two biggest steamboats Lake Minnetonka ever knew, and she ended her days as a dredge. The man who made her one was the same man who had helped build her.

Her name was the City of Saint Louis, and in her day she carried crowds. People rode the train out from Minneapolis and stepped aboard for the lake hotels, or for a picnic on Big Island. One lake historian counts some 50 big passenger boats on this water, and that's her in the painting, full at the dock.

The man was John Johnson. He came from Norway, helped build her, then captained her, and in time he owned her. Around the lake they called him the Sea Dog.

Then the railroad pushed west, and the tourists went with it. The old hotels closed or burned, but Johnson stayed. He bought up the steamers and turned them into dredges. Those were working boats that scooped mud and sand off the bottom, to deepen channels and build up shorelines. Where she had once carried picnickers, the City now dug the lake itself. Johnson worked this water the rest of his life, and his memorial in Excelsior still calls him Minnetonka's Sea Dog.

Chanhassen

Before it was Chanhassen, this town was St. Hubertus.

The parish out here started with a missionary priest, Father Mayr, who wanted German Catholic families to work, worship and send their kids to school side by side. In 1887 the Franciscan brothers who held the church land carried that idea forward. They laid out a little village right beside their new brick church and named it for the parish. The road out front was St. Hubert Street. For a few years, that was the name on the map.

Now, when the village incorporated, it went another way. It took the township's name instead, Chanhassen, from a Dakota word that loosely means maple sugar. So a town that began around a saint ended up named for the sweet sap of a tree.

And the church never moved. It still stands at W. 78th Street and Great Plains Boulevard, the old St. Hubert Street, under that same tall steeple, the oldest building in Chanhassen still on its original spot.

Tax-forfeiture auctions can create opportunities for investors, but they require verification beyond what appears in county tax records.

The issue was resolved, but it highlights an important lesson.
